We actually harvested our own DNA from cheek swabs, but the real excitement started when we realized we could use the results to compare our profiles with everyone else in the room. Suddenly, the whole lab turned into a giant guessing game as we tried to see who might actually share matching genetic traits. To understand race, it helps to look at our DNA. Scientists mapping the human genome discovered that all humans are 99.9% genetically identical. The tiny 0.1% difference accounts for external traits like skin tone, eye shape, and hair texture. However, these visible traits do not map onto distinct biological packages. A person can actually share more total DNA with someone of a different perceived race than with someone who looks just like them.

The racial categories we use today were actually invented a few hundred years ago. During the eras of European colonialism and the slave trade, people in power created a ranking system based on looks. They used these made-up categories to justify treating certain groups of people poorly and taking their land or labor. They picked visible traits like skin color to divide people, completely ignoring the complex biology happening beneath the surface.

Racial rules also change depending on where you are and what year it is. For instance, Irish and Italian immigrants in the United States were not always considered "White" in the 1800s, but over time, that definition shifted. Also, a person who is viewed as Black in the U.S. might be considered mixed-race in Brazil. Because biological traits do not change when you cross a border or step forward in time, these shifting definitions prove that race is an idea made up by society.

Even though race isn’t a real biological fact, it still has huge effects on people's real lives. Generations of treating people differently based on these categories have created unfair systems in housing, schools, and healthcare. When statistics show that certain racial groups face higher rates of sickness, it is almost never because of their DNA. Instead, it is usually because of the stress of facing discrimination and having less access to good medical care, proving that society creates these differences, not nature.
